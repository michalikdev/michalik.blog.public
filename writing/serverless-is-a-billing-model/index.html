Serverless is a billing model, not an architecture

The interesting property was never "no servers". It is that idle costs nothing — and every design consequence follows from that one line on the invoice.

In short

  • Serverless changes what you are charged for, not how the system is built: you pay per run instead of per hour.
  • That makes it excellent for anything that is idle most of the time — forms, webhooks, nightly jobs.
  • It is a poor fit for steady all-day load, long connections, or work that will not finish inside one invocation.
  • Paying nothing at idle and paying without limit under load are the same property, so put a cap on the account.

Everybody argues about serverless as though it were an architecture. It is not. It is a way of being charged, and almost every real consequence — good and bad — follows from that one fact rather than from anything technical.

A server you rent costs the same whether it serves ten requests a day or ten thousand. A function you deploy costs nothing until it runs. That is the entire difference, and it is a big one, but it is a difference on the invoice.

Once you see it that way, the arguments sort themselves out.

What the billing model gives you

If your traffic is spiky, bursty, or mostly nothing, you stop paying for the gaps. A contact form that gets fourteen submissions a week does not need a machine standing by all week. Neither does a webhook receiver, a scheduled report, or the little service that resizes an image when somebody uploads one.

The clearest win is the thing that runs rarely and must not be forgotten. Nobody wants to maintain a virtual machine whose entire job is to receive a form four times a day — and nobody does maintain it, which is how you end up with an unpatched box on the internet.

The second gift is that you stop operating a machine. No package updates, no disk filling up, no certificate expiring at two in the morning. That work does not disappear, but it moves to somebody whose full-time job it is.

What it takes away

  1. You cannot keep anything in memory

    A function may run in a fresh instance every time. Anything you want to remember has to be written somewhere that outlives the request, and that store is now a second thing to pay for, secure and reason about.

  2. Long work does not fit

    Every platform caps how long one invocation may run. Work that takes minutes has to be cut into pieces that hand off to each other, which is a real design cost and the usual reason a serverless project turns miserable.

  3. Local development stops being local

    The emulators are good and they are still emulators. The gap between "works on my machine" and "works deployed" is wider here than with a container you can run identically in both places.

  4. The bill has no ceiling

    Paying nothing at idle is the same property as paying without limit under load. A loop that calls itself, or a scraper that finds you, is a bill rather than a crash — and a crash is much easier to notice.

Where the line actually falls

The useful question is not "serverless or not". It is: does this thing spend most of its life doing nothing?

Shape of the workFitsWhy
Form handler, webhook receiverYesIdle almost always; each run is milliseconds
Scheduled report, nightly exportYesRuns briefly, then nothing for a day
API in front of a databaseMaybeConnection pooling is the whole problem — see below
Anything holding a long connectionNoThe model charges for time; long connections are all time
Steady, predictable, all-day loadNoA rented machine is cheaper and simpler at that point

The "maybe" row is where most people get hurt. A traditional database expects a handful of long-lived connections; a serverless platform can hand it hundreds of short ones at once, and the database falls over long before your function does. Every platform now has an answer — a proxy, a pooler, an HTTP-speaking database — and every one of those answers is another piece to run.

Paying nothing at idle and paying without limit under load are the same property, seen from two sides.

The honest summary

Serverless is excellent for the long tail of small things every organisation accumulates: the form, the hook, the nightly job, the glue. It is a poor fit for the one busy thing at the centre, which is exactly the thing people are most tempted to move first because it is the most expensive.

Start at the edges. Leave the middle alone until something forces you.

Questions

Is serverless cheaper than a virtual machine?

Only when the work is idle most of the time. A function that runs a few thousand times a month costs practically nothing, while a machine costs the same whether it works or not. Under steady all-day load the rented machine wins, and usually by a wide margin.

What happens if something calls my function in a loop?

You get an invoice rather than an outage, which is harder to notice. Every platform lets you cap spending or requests per day — set that limit on the first day, before there is anything worth attacking, because the default is unlimited.

Can I run a normal database behind serverless functions?

Yes, but connection pooling becomes the whole problem. Traditional databases expect a few long-lived connections and a serverless platform hands them hundreds of short ones. Use the platform's pooler or a database that speaks HTTP, and treat that extra piece as part of the cost.

Do cold starts still matter?

Much less than they did, and it depends entirely on the runtime. Platforms built on lightweight isolates start in single-digit milliseconds; container-based ones can still stall on the first request after a quiet period. If a delay of a second occasionally would hurt, measure it on your platform before designing around it.

Is serverless the same thing as edge?

No, though the same products often sell both. Serverless is about how you are charged and how instances appear; edge is about where the code runs geographically. A function can be serverless and run in one region, or run in fifty and be billed by the hour.
